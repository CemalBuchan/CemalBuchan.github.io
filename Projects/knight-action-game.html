Directional Combat Prototype · Unity / C#

MABW

A combat prototype that departs from traditional fighting games, built around reading enemy attacks in real time and reacting to them. Characters don't die — the goal is to gain the advantage by stunning your opponent through correct direction selection and perfect timing.

Overview

MABW is an attempt to build a reflex-driven combat system that rewards observation and timing over trading damage. The enemy telegraphs its attack by randomly committing to a direction and winding up; the player's job is to read that tell and react in time.

Reading the correct direction at the right moment stuns the opponent and opens a punish window; a wrong or late reaction leaves the player exposed instead. The result is a fast, reflex-driven combat loop that rewards observation and timing over raw mechanical execution.

Systems I built

  • Attack-Mode State Machine — holding Ctrl enters Attack Mode; while active, right-click selects the attack direction and left-click executes the attack.
  • Direction-Based Input System — input logic that compares the player's chosen direction against the enemy's to determine the stun/damage outcome.
  • Stun System (Core Mechanic) — if the enemy attacks from the direction the player selected, the enemy is stunned; if the player attacks from the direction the enemy selected, the player is stunned instead.
  • Damage Resolution Logic — attacking from a direction the enemy didn't select deals damage; attacking from the enemy's selected direction still deals damage if the enemy is already stunned — requiring the player to evaluate the enemy's stun state, not just the direction.
  • Enemy-Player Interaction Logic — the enemy's behavior of randomly committing to a direction and winding up gives the player a readable tell.
  • Sprint & Movement System — a basic movement system with Shift-triggered sprint for increased speed.

What I took away from this project

This project taught me a lot about state machine design, synchronizing input-driven systems, and the timing precision reflex-based gameplay demands. Building the Attack Mode transitions and direction-comparison logic end-to-end gave me a much stronger grasp of state management. Designing the enemy's pre-attack "tell" taught me that AI needs to feel both readable and fair — signal too early and it becomes predictable, too late and it feels unfair, and I had to find that fine timing balance. Coding the stun/damage resolution logic also showed me that a rule that looks simple on paper (right direction vs. wrong direction) actually has to evaluate multiple states at once (direction, timing, stun status) — an experience that sharpened my sense for reactive system design.
